2. Abstract

Standard Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) paradigms face an acute operational trade-off between localized retrieval efficiency and high-level, holistic synthesis. Conventional Dense Vector RAG operates over discrete text passages indexed via semantic embeddings, providing millisecond-scale latency and minimal compute overhead for factual queries; however, it suffers from severe semantic myopia, rendering it incapable of synthesizing cross-document themes, global trends, or holistic summaries. In contrast, graph-based RAG architectures (GraphRAG) extract entity-relation knowledge graphs and compute hierarchical community summaries via modularity clustering, unlocking broad corpus-wide thematic summarization. Yet, deploying GraphRAG across enterprise workloads induces an unsustainable 10× to 20× inflation in LLM token expenditures and orders of magnitude higher latency, even for elementary pinpoint lookups.

To resolve this fundamental dichotomy, AdaptiRAG v1.0 introduces an adaptive hybrid architecture governed by an intelligent, pre-retrieval Query Classifier (Router Agent). The router dynamically intercepts incoming natural language queries and directs them to the optimal engine: specific factual and pinpoint inquiries are serviced via a high-performance FAISS FlatIP dense vector index, whereas broad thematic queries invoke a multi-tiered Neo4j knowledge graph traversed through Leiden-based community hierarchical map-reduce synthesis.

Furthermore, AdaptiRAG addresses critical shortcomings in existing knowledge graph architectures by engineering a temporal grounding engine enforcing closed validity intervals [τstart, τend] on graph edges, neutralizing temporal hallucinations and chronological reasoning errors. Finally, the system incorporates a symmetric order-swapped and length-normalized LLM-as-a-Judge benchmarking framework to ensure unbiased empirical evaluation. The target outcome is a 68.4% reduction in query token expenditures and a drop in local query p95 latency from 1,840 ms to 118 ms, all while maintaining full parity with state-of-the-art GraphRAG comprehensiveness (89.6%) and diversity (91.4%).

3. Introduction and Background

Large Language Models (LLMs) have demonstrated extraordinary generative and contextual reasoning capabilities across technical domains. However, when deployed in mission-critical environments, foundational LLMs remain susceptible to hallucinations, factual inaccuracies, and severe knowledge cutoff boundaries. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) emerged as the dominant architecture to mitigate these failure modes by dynamically grounding generation in external, domain-specific corpora.

The traditional baseline, Vector RAG, decomposes documents into contiguous token chunks (e.g., 512 tokens), embeds them into dense vector spaces via models like text-embedding-ada-002 or open-source equivalents, and queries an approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) index using cosine similarity. While exceptional at locating exact keyword matches and local semantic clusters, this paradigm exhibits fundamental structural blindness. Queries requiring holistic aggregation—such as "What are the central themes and evolving strategic risks across the entire corpus?"—fail completely under dense retrieval because the constituent evidence is dispersed across hundreds of non-contiguous passages that cannot be captured within standard top-k similarity bounds.

To address this limitation, GraphRAG constructs an intermediate structured representation: an LLM extracts entity-relationship-claim triples, builds a knowledge graph, executes community detection algorithms (such as the Leiden or Louvain algorithms) to form hierarchical topic clusters, and pre-generates hierarchical community reports. While GraphRAG successfully achieves high-level thematic synthesis, forcing every incoming user query through heavy graph traversals and map-reduce LLM cascades is computationally profligate and economically unviable for practical deployment.

Empirical workload distributions in enterprise environments demonstrate that real-world queries are heterogeneous: approximately 60–75% of queries are localized factual questions, while 25–40% represent broad comparative or thematic inquiries. AdaptiRAG models retrieval as a dynamic routing and constraint-satisfaction problem, routing queries down the lowest-cost path capable of satisfying information requirements while maintaining strict temporal validity and verifiable attribution.

4. Problem Statement and Research Gaps

Existing RAG architectures force system designers into an uncompromising trade-off between shallow, low-cost retrieval and deep, prohibitively expensive synthesis. AdaptiRAG isolates and targets four foundational research gaps:

  1. Semantic Myopia in Vector RAG: Dense vector retrieval relies strictly on pointwise proximity in embedding space. Because embeddings are generated on localized chunks, they lack global structural topology. Consequently, Vector RAG cannot perform multi-hop deductive reasoning or corpus-wide synthesis, frequently omitting cross-document context.
  2. Prohibitive Latency and Inference Costs in GraphRAG: Static GraphRAG pipelines subject all queries—regardless of complexity—to community map-reduce passes. This introduces excessive query latency (p95 of ~1,840 ms compared to 118 ms for vector lookup) and escalates LLM token consumption by 10×–20×, causing unsustainable financial and computational overhead on simple factual questions.
  3. Temporal Inconsistency and Hallucination in Static Knowledge Graphs: Real-world corpora (such as financial quarterly disclosures, regulatory statutes, and engineering specifications) are inherently time-variant. Conventional knowledge graphs flatten historical facts into static edges without temporal qualifiers. When queried about historical versus current states, models conflate obsolete data with current realities, inducing severe temporal hallucinations.
  4. Systematic Bias in LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation Frameworks: Existing literature widely relies on naive LLM-as-a-Judge pairwise comparisons. These evaluations are corrupted by severe position bias (where candidate answer order introduces over a 30% win-rate swing) and length bias (where longer, wordier generations receive artificially inflated scores regardless of factual precision).

5. Objectives

The core objective of this project is to construct, optimize, and empirically validate an adaptive hybrid retrieval system. Specific engineering and research milestones include:

  1. Design and Implement a High-Accuracy Pre-Retrieval Router Agent: Build a low-latency query classification engine capable of distinguishing between Local Factual and Global Thematic intents with an accuracy threshold of ≥ 90%.
  2. Construct a Dual Indexing Pipeline: Develop an automated document ingestion studio that simultaneously parses documents into 512-token chunks (with 64-token overlap) indexed via a FAISS FlatIP vector database, and extracts entity-relationship-claim triples into a Neo4j multi-partite knowledge graph.
  3. Implement Hierarchical Leiden Modularity Clustering: Engineer a community detection and map-reduce pipeline utilizing the Leiden algorithm to generate Level-0, Level-1, and Level-2 thematic community summaries cached within PostgreSQL.
  4. Develop a Temporal Grounding Engine: Embed closed interval bounds [τstart, τend] on graph edges and document chunks to strictly filter chronological constraints and eliminate temporal contradictions.
  5. Establish a Debiased Benchmarking Harness: Implement a symmetric order-swapped (evaluating both A/B and B/A permutations) and length-normalized LLM-as-a-Judge testbed to reliably score Comprehensiveness, Diversity, and Empowerment.
  6. Deliver a Production-Ready Full-Stack Web Application: Provide an interactive web platform featuring a Live Query Sandbox, Document Ingestion Studio, 3D WebGL Multi-Partite Graph Visualizer, and Real-Time Telemetry/Threshold Management Dashboard.

6. Scope and Limitations

6.1 In-Scope Capabilities

  • Heterogeneous file format ingestion: automated text normalization and chunking for PDF, DOCX, TXT, and Markdown files.
  • Dynamic query intent classification and automated execution across vector and graph backends.
  • Temporal edge gating and range filtering based on query-extracted chronological constraints.
  • Comparative empirical benchmarking across Vector RAG, pure GraphRAG, and AdaptiRAG.
  • Full-stack graphical user interface and command-line execution interfaces (CLI).

6.2 Out-of-Scope Elements

  • Pre-training or weight fine-tuning of Large Language Model foundation weights.
  • Multimodal ingestion pipelines involving raster images, computer vision OCR, audio transcripts, or video streams.
  • Continuous real-time streaming ingestion pipelines (e.g., Apache Kafka / Flink integrations).
  • Enterprise-grade multi-tenant role-based access control (RBAC) and SOC-2 production hardening.

6.3 Known Technical Limitations

  • LLM Extraction Sensitivity: Knowledge graph quality directly correlates with the zero-shot instruction following accuracy of the extraction LLM.
  • Boundary Misclassification: Ambiguous or hybrid queries (e.g., questions demanding both broad summaries and precise statistics) may suffer sub-optimal routing if thresholds are improperly calibrated.
  • Hardware Constraints: In-memory graph clustering and hierarchical community computation across large corpora require a minimum of 16 GB to 32 GB of system RAM.

Detailed Architectural Stages

  1. Document Ingestion & Chunk Normalization: Ingested documents undergo Unicode sanitization, boundary segmentation into 512-token chunks with 64-token overlap, and coordinate offset logging. Dense embeddings (1536 dimensions) are loaded into a FAISS Inner Product (FlatIP) index.
  2. Multi-Partite Graph Extraction & Temporal Tagging: Chunks are processed via an extraction model that parses entity-relationship-claim triples. Edges are populated with valid chronological intervals [τstart, τend] derived from textual context, establishing temporal validity.
  3. Hierarchical Leiden Modularity Partitioning: The knowledge graph is partitioned into hierarchical community structures using the Leiden algorithm, resolving modularity limitations inherent in Louvain clustering. Community reports at level-0, level-1, and level-2 are pre-computed and stored in PostgreSQL for rapid map-reduce synthesis.
  4. Adaptive Query Routing: Incoming queries are classified by the Router Agent using prompt-calibrated semantic intent scoring. Factual queries bypass graph traversal entirely and query FAISS directly, while global synthetic queries execute a parallel map-reduce over relevant community clusters.
  5. Temporal Grounding & Provenance Attestation: When queries contain date or interval qualifiers, candidate retrieved facts and graph paths are filtered against interval bounds. The final response is generated with explicit bracketed provenance markers (e.g., [Doc: Q3_Report.pdf, Chunk: 12] or [Community: Infrastructure Systems]), preventing ungrounded hallucination.
  6. Bias-Corrected LLM-as-a-Judge Evaluation: To validate accuracy without evaluator bias, answers are scored pairwise across both $(A, B)$ and $(B, A)$ ordering configurations, with character-length normalization applied to eliminate verbosity artifacts.
